The problem

A rating system where nobody ever rates anything badly

A five-star scale assumes a random sample of opinion. Recipe sites get the opposite. You browse, you pick the dish you already like the sound of, you cook it, and only then do you rate. Everyone who would have scored it a 2 never made it in the first place.

The result is compression. Thousands of recipes crowd into the top half-star, ordering becomes arbitrary, and the rating stops carrying information — which is exactly the failure Beli fixed for restaurants by refusing to accept a number at all.

The mechanic

One question, then a few duels

The whole product is three interactions. None of them is “rate this out of five.”

01

Log what you cooked

Import from a TikTok, an Instagram reel or an NYT link, or just type it. The import is the acquisition loop and the overlap engine at once — everyone is cooking the same few hundred viral recipes, which is what makes friend comparison possible at all.

02

Would you make it again?

Yes, maybe, or never. Three bands, nothing finer.

This is the load-bearing choice. It’s the real decision a cook makes, and it’s honest about execution — if your carbonara broke, you won’t make it again, and that’s a data point rather than noise to be argued away.

03

Two dishes at a time

Inside the band, the new dish is placed by binary search against what’s already there. Each answer halves the remaining range.

The score is then derived from final position, never entered. That single design choice is what makes grade inflation structurally impossible.

Comparisons needed to place one dish

Binary search means the work grows logarithmically, so the mechanic gets cheaper per dish exactly as your rotation gets valuable enough to be worth ranking.

View as table

Exact values, not modelled: ceil(log₂(n + 1)) comparisons to insert into a sorted band of n dishes. A hundred-dish rotation is still seven taps.

Why cooking

Five things a ranking app needs. Cooking is one of the few that has all of them.

“People do it daily” is not the bar — people commute daily and nobody wants a commute-ranking app. The bar is a recurring decision, with divergent taste, made with or for other people, over a set that’s dense locally and infinite globally, attached to money. Two adjacent ideas fail it on frequency alone.

The landscape

Everyone built a diary. Nobody built the opinion.

The space is crowding — CookDiary shipped in 2026, and a wave of Letterboxd-for-food apps arrived alongside it. All of them log. None of them rank. Structurally, this is where restaurants sat right before Beli.

The honest part

Three things that could kill this

A pitch that only argues the upside isn’t worth reading. These are the real objections, in the order they’d actually bite.

Risk 01 — structural

The item isn’t fixed

Carbone is Carbone for everyone, which is why Beli’s rankings aggregate into something meaningful. A recipe is mediated by your own execution. If the carbonara was bad, was that the recipe or was that you?

That noise sits directly in the signal, and it breaks cross-user aggregation in a way the restaurant case never has to handle.

The answer“Would you make it again” already absorbs execution — you’re rating the decision to repeat, not the platonic recipe. Aggregate on repeat rate rather than score, and the noise mostly cancels.
Risk 02 — network

Friend overlap is thin

Restaurants force overlap through geography — you and your friends are in the same city eating at the same hundred places. A recipe catalogue is global and effectively infinite, so two people may share almost nothing.

Without overlap there is no taste distance, and without taste distance the friend graph is decoration.

The answerWedge on viral recipes. Everyone cooks the same few hundred TikTok and NYT dishes — that is the dense local set, and one-tap import is both the acquisition loop and the overlap engine.
Risk 03 — competitive

Pepper can ship this as a feature

Pepper already owns the two genuinely hard assets: a social graph of cooks and a working one-tap import pipeline from TikTok, Instagram and Pinterest.

An opinion layer on top of that is a sprint, not a company. If this thesis is right, they are the most likely party to prove it.

The answerSpeed, and the fact that ranking is a different product posture from a feed — it needs cold-start comparisons, not engagement. Feed companies rarely ship mechanics that reduce time in app.
